# Geological Writing Voice

**Use it when** writing or revising copy that describes rocks, time, plate motion, fossils, or landscapes for a reader who is not a geologist.

**Skip it for** code comments, commit messages, data schemas, and citations.

## Where the voice comes from

**Tanya Atwater** writes plate motion as motion. Plates and continents are objects with direction, speed, and a history of collisions, and she uses ordinary physical verbs for what they do: slide, tear, jam, rotate, drag. She gives the reader a vantage point, usually a place they already know, riding on top of the moving thing. Take from her the habit of putting the reader somewhere specific and letting the ground move underneath them.

**Sudipta Sengupta** writes from the outcrop. Geometry gets stated precisely before it is interpreted: which way a fold closes, how a second deformation cuts across the first, what the dip was in degrees. Her field writing lets the observer into the sentence when the observer explains how the observation was made. Take from her the discipline of describing the shape before naming the process.

**Marcia Bjornerud** writes time as duration rather than as a large number. She picks up one rock or one layer and unpacks how long it took to form, and her analogies come from handled objects such as a palimpsest, a ledger, a scar. She uses one analogy at a time and then drops it. Take from her the move of converting an interval into something a body can register.

## Sentence rules

**Lead with the observation.** State what is in the rock, the map, or the reconstruction, then say what it means. Two sentences is usually the right split.

**Use physical verbs for geological processes.** Rifts open, basins fill, arcs collide, sediment buries, faults lock and slip. Avoid abstract nouns doing the work, as in "the occurrence of deposition" or "a period of uplift activity."

**Anchor every scale.** Pair a number with something the reader can stand next to: a distance in city blocks, a thickness in stories, a rate in millimeters per year set against fingernail growth. One anchor per passage, because a second one competes with the first.

**Name the place in modern terms first.** "The rock that is now Vermont" reads better for a general audience than "the eastern Laurentian margin." Give the technical name afterward in parentheses when it is worth having.

**State uncertainty in plain words.** "Estimates range from 8 to 12 million years." "The position of this block before the Jurassic is poorly constrained." Skip hedging adverbs such as arguably, notably, and relatively.

**Use first person only when the observer matters to the claim.** If the sentence works without you in it, stay out of it.

**Vary sentence length on purpose, and keep the long ones structurally simple.** Subordinate clauses should stack forward rather than nest inside each other.

## Punctuation without the em dash

The em dash is out of the voice entirely. When a draft wants one, replace it with whichever of these fits:

1. **A period.** Two sentences almost always work, and this is the right answer most of the time.
2. **A semicolon**, when the two halves are genuinely balanced independent clauses.
3. **A pair of commas** for a short aside of five words or fewer.
4. **Parentheses** for a technical name, a date range, or a measurement.
5. **A colon**, used sparingly, when a list or a specification follows.

Do not substitute an en dash or a spaced hyphen, which read as the same gesture. Do not use ellipses for pauses. Keep the hyphen for compound modifiers only (fine-grained, north-dipping, 30-centimeter).

## Constructions to cut

**Antithesis.** Remove every "this is this, not that" and its family: "not X, but Y," "less a X than a Y," "X isn't just Y, it's Z," "more than just." Say the affirmative thing once. If a contrast genuinely carries information, give the wrong idea its own sentence and then correct it in the next one.

**The rule of three.** Triads of adjectives, triads of clauses, triads of examples. Use two or four instead, and let the count be set by how many real items there are.

**Rhetorical questions as transitions.** "So what does this tell us?" Delete the question and state the finding.

**Throat-clearing openings.** "In the world of plate tectonics," "It's important to understand that," "Imagine a world where," "Picture this." Start with the subject of the sentence.

**Elevated closers.** Do not end a paragraph or a section on a summarizing line that reaches for significance. End on the last piece of information. A section that feels unfinished without a closer has its information in the wrong order.

**Stock vocabulary.** delve, tapestry, testament to, landscape of, journey, unlock, harness, realm, profound, remarkable, fascinating, intricate dance, weaves together, lies at the heart of, paints a picture, serves as a reminder.

**Scare quotes around a term you just invented.** Either the term is established and takes no quotes, or you define it in a clause.

**Hedged both-sides endings.** "Whatever the case, the story is still being written." "Only time will tell."

**Fragments for emphasis.** A one-word sentence standing alone after a full one. Write the full sentence.

## Applying it to the deep-time globe

**Period descriptions.** Open with where the continents were, then climate, then life. Four or five sentences, so the panel does not scroll. Give one date range in the first sentence and no others.

**Site blurbs (fossils, forests, poles).** Four moves in order: name the feature, give its age, say what the place was like at the time, say where that rock sits today. The last move is what makes the marker worth tapping.

**Layer captions.** One sentence of composition, one sentence of the environment that produced it. Include a thickness or a rate whenever the data supports one.

**Comparison copy (inherited versus convergent resemblances).** Describe each place on its own terms first, one or two sentences each. Then state the shared feature and whether it comes from common ancestry or from similar conditions acting separately. The comparison belongs in the third position, not as the frame for the whole passage, and the word "versus" stays out of the reader-facing copy.

**Interface microcopy.** Labels, tooltips, and button text stay literal. "Jump to 250 Ma" rather than "Travel back in time." No exclamation points.

## Self-check before shipping

Run this over the draft. Any box left unticked means a revision.

- [ ] Zero em dashes, en dashes used as punctuation, or spaced hyphens.
- [ ] Zero "not X, but Y" constructions in any of their forms.
- [ ] Every large number carries an anchor, and no passage carries two.
- [ ] Each paragraph ends on information rather than on a summarizing line.
- [ ] Physical verbs are doing the work, with no abstract process nouns left standing.
- [ ] No word from the stock vocabulary list survives.
- [ ] Uncertainty, where it exists, is stated as a range or a plain sentence.
- [ ] Read aloud: no sentence lands like the end of a paragraph in a magazine profile.

One extra pass worth doing: search the draft for "is" and "was" and see how many sentences rest on them. Two or three per paragraph is fine, and more than that usually means the verbs got replaced by descriptions.
